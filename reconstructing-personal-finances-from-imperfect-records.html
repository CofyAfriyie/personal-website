When Money Moves Before It Is Spent

I began with one question: where did my money go? A four-day audit became a practical method for using statements, screenshots, event notes, and better controls without pretending uncertainty does not exist.

Afriyie Kofi Owusu-Ansah10 min readPersonal finance

In 2025, I tried to audit my own finances.

The plan was noble. I would gather the statements, make a few sensible charts, and emerge as the kind of person who says, “My cash flow tells a compelling story.”

Instead, I discovered that my records had the emotional range of a drawer full of receipts.

Some transactions were clear. Others were scattered across bank statements, mobile-money statements, payment confirmations, screenshots, cash withdrawals, and the increasingly ambitious version of my own memory. I did not have the right tools or a dependable way to join the pieces. The audit stalled.

This year, I returned with more patience and several tools, especially Claude and Codex. The first full audit cycle began on a Friday evening and reached its first completed version by Monday afternoon. It was an intense four days. It also used up my weekly Claude allowance, which felt dramatic at the time but was worth it.

Claude helped push the first implementation forward. Codex became the heavyweight for evidence review, controls, and the awkward questions that arrive after a spreadsheet says it is finished. When I use “we” in this article, I mean that small team: me, Claude, and Codex.

The question stayed simple: where did my money go?

The answer became more interesting than a budget. It became a reconstruction project.

Personal finance changed for me the moment I stopped asking whether every line was categorised and started asking whether every number could explain itself. Where did it come from? What did it represent? Was it spending, a transfer, cash waiting to be spent, or something I could not yet prove?

This is both the story of that audit and a guide for you if you want to attempt your own. The examples are fictionalized. I have removed private amounts, identities, institutions, and transaction details. I am not claiming a perfect reconciliation. The point is to show a useful method, including what to do when some answers remain open.

The cash withdrawal that became a plot twist

At first, cash looked easy. Money left an account. I had cash. Surely that was spending.

Not quite.

Imagine you withdraw cash on the last day of June. You use some of it immediately, keep some for a few days, and pay for a repair in July. If your report records the whole withdrawal as June spending, June looks busier than it really was and July quietly loses a real expense.

That is not a minor filing issue. It changes the story your monthly report tells.

I learned to separate four different dates:

DateWhat it answers
Withdrawal dateWhen did cash leave an account or wallet?
Spending dateWhen did a purchase or payment actually happen?
Transfer dateWhen did money move between your own accounts or platforms?
Reporting periodWhich month should the item appear in under your documented policy?

The dates may match. Often, they do not. A statement may carry a posting date while the purchase happened earlier. A mobile-money transfer can fund cash that is used later. A transfer can move money without creating income or consumption.

There is no single date rule that suits every purpose. A spending report may use the purchase date. A cash-position report may focus on the movement date. A statement reconciliation may use the posting date. Pick a policy, write it down, and explain any exception.

The first major lesson was this: a cash withdrawal is usually a funding movement into physical cash. It becomes an expense when you have evidence of its final use.

Build a source pack before you build a dashboard

Dashboards are tempting. They have colours. They have percentages. They can make adulthood feel only three charts away.

But a dashboard cannot repair a weak record underneath it.

I began with a source pack instead. If you are doing this yourself, collect the records before you start classifying them:

  • bank and mobile-money statements for the period you are reviewing;
  • card, wallet, investment, or payment-platform exports where relevant;
  • invoices, order histories, payment confirmations, and receipts;
  • screenshots that confirm a payment, transaction, ride, or order when the main export is incomplete;
  • a simple event and context record: dates you attended something, travel connected to it, or a payment that you can later explain; and
  • your own recollection, clearly labelled as recollection rather than quietly presented as a statement record.

The hierarchy matters. A statement line is usually stronger evidence than a remembered detail. A payment screenshot can corroborate a transaction that does not appear in an export. An event note can explain why a ride or purchase is plausible on a given day. Context is valuable, but it is not a substitute for evidence.

For example, a fictional Saturday event may leave several clues: a ride receipt, a food payment, and a transfer arriving later that covered part of the evening. The event note helps you investigate the cluster. It does not, on its own, prove that every transaction nearby belongs to that event. I looked for date, time, amount, direction, and a source connection before drawing a conclusion.

The FDIC’s personal-finance materials recommend tracking income and expenses, reviewing actual records each month, and keeping support for important expenses. Its spending-and-saving-plan guide is a useful starting point: compare the plan with evidence of what actually happened.

Give every record a route back home

Once I had the source material, I built a normalized ledger. Each entry needed enough information to survive a question later: date, amount, account or payment method, category, source reference, a stable transaction ID, and a confidence label when the classification was not direct.

The aim was a traceable route backwards:

dashboard total → category total → ledger entry → original source

That chain became the project’s quiet backbone. A total is much less mysterious when it can show its working.

If you are using a spreadsheet, do not make the summary your source of truth. Let the detailed ledger be the source of truth. A monthly report, category table, or dashboard should calculate from that detail. Then a correction belongs on the ledger line, where its evidence is visible, rather than in a lonely summary cell with no explanation.

Treat internal transfers as paired movements

Internal transfers are an easy way to accidentally give the same money two careers.

If you move money from one of your own accounts to another, one side shows money leaving and the other shows money arriving. If those rows are treated as independent events, the spreadsheet can label one as spending and the other as income. The total activity rises. Nothing has actually been earned or consumed.

My control was simple enough for a sticky note: give both sides the same transfer ID.

For your own audit, a transfer ID lets you ask:

  • Is there a matching incoming or outgoing side?
  • Are the amount and direction compatible?
  • Is the timing understood?
  • Has the movement been excluded from both income and consumption spending?

If you cannot find the other side, do not force a category. Put it in a review queue. A question mark is more useful than a confident misclassification.

Use people and events as context, not shortcuts

One of the project’s more useful working tools was a private counterparty directory. It did not expose anyone publicly. It simply recorded what had already been established: a recurring payment destination, a known relationship where confirmed, prior classifications, and the evidence behind them.

That matters because a repeated number or account can be a clue. Suppose the waakye seller's number appears in several already-confirmed food payments. A new payment to the same merchant may deserve a probable food label while you investigate it. It does not become confirmed merely because the number looks familiar.

The same caution applies to events. A known event on a certain date can make a nearby ride or purchase worth checking. It should not be used to rewrite a conflicting statement line or fill every gap by proximity.

My practical rule was: let recurring patterns raise or lower confidence, never replace proof. I used four labels—confirmed, strong, probable, and unresolved—so the file could say exactly what the evidence supported. If the evidence was weak, the classification stayed weak. That is not a defect. It is honest bookkeeping.

Let unassigned cash stay visible

The least glamorous category turned out to be one of the most important: unassigned cash.

It represents money whose withdrawal is visible but whose final use is not yet supported. The old instinct would have been to send it into “miscellaneous” and keep the report moving. But miscellaneous is where uncertainty goes to become invisible.

Instead, I treated unassigned cash as a visible status. Cash could be known, unassigned, or documented as still held. That created a deceptively useful identity:

cash withdrawn = known cash uses + unassigned cash + documented cash still held

If that identity fails, something may have been double-counted, omitted, or assigned inconsistently. If it holds, it does not prove every category is right, but it gives you a firm place to begin your next review.

You need a clear policy for physical cash at period-end. Will you carry it forward as cash held? Will you leave it unassigned until it is allocated? Either can be reasonable when explained. What you cannot do is count the same money as both cash on hand and an expense.

Make checks run without your memory

The project produced reports and summaries, but its most useful output was the set of controls that made those reports less fragile.

You can apply the same approach in a modest spreadsheet. Check source coverage: did every relevant statement line, screenshot, or receipt enter your ledger, or have a documented reason not to? Check transaction IDs for duplicates. Pair internal transfers. Test the cash identity. Check that report totals trace back to detailed rows.

These are not impressive party tricks. They work when your memory is tired, when a record arrives late, or when you discover that a purchase appeared in both a receipt collection and a statement export.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau makes a related point from a consumer-protection angle: monitor account activity and retain payment records, including the amount and timing of automatic payments. Its guidance matters because timing and supporting records are part of the evidence, not administrative clutter.

The most useful checks are the ones that run without you remembering them. A formula can flag a broken cash identity. A duplicate-ID test can reveal a repeated transaction. A transfer-pair check can surface a movement with only one side recorded. Five minutes of structured checks is far kinder than a weekend of financial archaeology.

Keep a changelog when the story changes

The change log became the project’s memory.

When a classification changed, I recorded what changed, why it changed, what evidence supported it, and which reports were affected. A fictional example might be a purchase first treated as cash because no electronic payment was visible, then reclassified when a payment confirmation surfaced. Another might be a suspected duplicate discovered because the same order had been represented in two different sources.

The correction is not just a new number. It is an explanation of how the new number was reached.

This prevents the next review from repeating the same investigation. It also protects you from a dashboard that quietly becomes a new reality without showing how it got there.

Formulas helped, but they were not magic

One recurring problem was the hardcoded summary number: a total typed manually after a calculation, correct in that exact moment and quietly stale after the next correction.

Where the inputs were structured, I replaced those snapshots with formulas. Category totals calculated from ledger detail. Monthly views calculated from dates and the agreed reporting policy. Cash controls calculated from withdrawals and allocations.

This makes reports more resilient. Correct a source row, and the summary follows it.

But formulas are not magic. A formula can calculate incomplete data very efficiently. It can calculate a duplicated transaction with exquisite confidence. It can faithfully report a category that was guessed incorrectly in the first place.

That is why the evidence layer comes first. Formulas tell you whether the report follows the ledger. Evidence tells you whether the ledger follows reality.

The Federal Reserve’s household well-being research continues to examine income, expenses, savings, banking, credit, and financial fragility. Its current survey is a reminder that a useful personal-finance system is not one that merely looks polished. It is one that helps you understand what is happening and what remains uncertain.

The ending is not perfectly tidy

I began this audit because I wanted a clearer answer to one simple question: where did my money go?

I have a much better answer now. It is traceable, more consistent, and far less dependent on memory. It separates cash withdrawals from spending, treats transfers as movements rather than new income, and lets reports update from the underlying detail.

And then there is the small transaction that still will not identify itself.

After the statements, screenshots, event notes, transfer links, duplicate checks, and change-log entries, one modest payment remains stubbornly unexplained. I have searched for it. The file has searched for it. It has not suddenly become a meal, transport, income, or a transfer merely because I would prefer a cleaner ending.

So it stays in the review queue.

It is not perfect. Some items remain open. I am comfortable saying that because reconciliation is not a beauty contest. You do not win by forcing every record into a category until the report looks finished. You win by making the known parts trustworthy and the unknown parts visible.

That is when personal finance became more than budgeting for me. It became a practice of building evidence, asking better questions, and leaving a clear trail for the next answer.

A monthly routine you can keep

At month-end, keep the routine small:

  1. Gather statements, mobile-money records, exports, receipts, and screenshots. Retain the originals.
  2. Bring relevant records into one ledger and attach a source reference to each row.
  3. Link internal transfers on both sides.
  4. Allocate only cash uses that the evidence supports. Leave the rest unassigned with a next review action.
  5. Run source, ID, transfer, cash, and report checks before trusting the dashboard.
  6. Add every meaningful correction to your change log.

The aim is not a spreadsheet that looks finished. It is a financial record that remains useful when the next question arrives.

The best part is that you can now have AI help. You may not need both Claude and Codex as I did. Plan carefully before you begin, though, or you may burn through your token allowance faster than you expect.
